Kurzweil Reading Machine

Detailed Description:
The original Kurzweil Reading Machine is a large, stand-alone machine about the size of a small dishwasher. Its scanner section alone, held by the Computer History Museum, is about 19 inches wide, 12 inches high and 20 inches long.
On top is a glass plate. You place a book, magazine or typed page face down on the glass, as on a photocopier, and close a cover over it.
Inside, a CCD optical scanner driven by the built-in minicomputer finds the first line of print, scans it, then moves to the next line and continues to the end of the page. The machine handles most common typefaces but not handwriting, decorative type or poor printing.
The user controls it from a separate keypad. At NFB's suggestion the keys had no braille labels, and a Nominator key let you press any key to hear what it did. The keypad also controlled reading by word, line or spelling.
The speech came from its own text-to-speech system, a robotic but understandable voice reading about 200 words per minute. The 1976 press release said only basic familiarity with its 'accent' was needed.

History:
Ray Kurzweil built the KRM by combining three inventions: omni-font OCR, the CCD flatbed scanner and text-to-speech. The idea came after a blind man on a plane told him that print was his only real barrier. The prototype was finished in late 1975 and announced with the NFB on January 13, 1976; Walter Cronkite used it to read his sign-off on CBS. NFB scientists asked for no braille labels and for a 'Nominator' key that announces what other keys do. A federal contract placed units at Perkins and in the Boston schools.
Successors: K-NFB Reader (K-NFB Reading Technology, 2005)
