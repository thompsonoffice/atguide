Detailed Description:
The SynPhonix 200 is an internal expansion card for the IBM PC and compatibles; it has no external box. SpeechChips.com describes it as a board carrying its own speaker, with the SSI 263 phoneme synthesizer chip on the card.
Its connections are on the card's rear bracket, at the back of the computer. SpeechChips.com describes an external-speaker connection and a volume control there, and Trace Center's 1987 Resource Book 3 says voice output could go through the SynPhonix speaker or any external speaker.
Because the speaker is on the board, the card can talk with nothing plugged into it, though sound then comes from inside the computer case. Plugging in an external speaker moves the voice outside the case; a contemporary review cited by SpeechChips.com used that output to record spoken material onto cassette.
Speech settings (rate, pitch, amplitude) are set in software, not with knobs; the only physical control is the rear volume control. Trace listed the card at $225 in 1987, with text-to-speech, demonstration and fixed-vocabulary programs included.
No dimensions, weight or board-length figure for the SynPhonix 200 was found in the sources checked this session (Trace's 1987 and 1988 Resource Books, SpeechChips.com and Artic's own history pages give none). Related boards fitted other computers: the SynPhonix 100 for the Apple II and the SynPhonix jr for the PCjr.

History:
Artic Technologies was founded in 1984 by Art Velthoven, Dale McDaniel, Kathy Gargagliano and Tim Gargagliano. All four came from the research and development group at Votrax, the speech synthesis subsidiary of Federal Screw Works. In Artic's own history, the SynPhonix 200 card, released in 1985, was the company's first commercial product; SpeechChips.com notes that the SynPhonix family was already being advertised for IBM and Apple computers in 1984. The card fit inside an IBM PC and spoke through the 263 phoneme chip (the SSI 263, originally Votrax's SC-02), which built words from individual speech sounds. Artic recalls building synthesizers in the founders' basements and moving offices several times in those early years. The 200 became an important partner for screen-reading software, and in 1987 it was replaced by the SynPhonix 215.
Successors: SynPhonix 215 (Artic Technologies, 1987)
