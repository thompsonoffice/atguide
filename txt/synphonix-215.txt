Detailed Description:
The SynPhonix 215 is an internal card for the IBM PC family and the PS/2 Models 25 and 30. Trace's 1996-97 ResourceBook explains that the 215 is the SynPhonix 210 board bundled with Artic's VEST text-to-speech rules and either Artic Vision ($795) or Business Vision ($895), so physically it is the 210 card.
Trace says the voice can come through the on-board speaker, a small external speaker, or headphones, all three included, and that the volume and treble controls are 'located externally'.
The card also has a built-in Artic Switch Interface, so that switches can be plugged into the computer for use with Artic's screen-access programs. An optional accessory for the 215 was Turbo-Pedals ($150), foot pedals giving fast-forward, rewind, review mode and instant quiet.
SpeechChips.com photographs a related Artic board marked 'SP-21X Revision 3' (©1987): a large on-board loudspeaker dominates the card, two adjustment controls and a multi-pin connector sit on the rear bracket, and jumpers labelled MAP and IRQ provide configuration. It is described as belonging to the 21x family, not identified specifically as a 215.
No dimensions or weight for the 215 were found in the sources checked. Artic's own history says the 210, 215 and 216 ISA cards are all out of production; the Micro Channel equivalents for PS/2 Models 50 and above were the SynPhonix 310 and 315.

History:
Artic released the SynPhonix 215 in 1987, the year it moved into its own building in Troy, Michigan. It replaced the 200, and Artic later described it as still known for its fast speech rate, accurate text-to-speech, responsiveness, and instant silencing. It was usually paired with Artic's Business Vision screen reader (1987). AccessWorld noted that SynPhonix and Artic's screen readers were significant, lower-cost competitors to DECtalk and JAWS. Related ISA cards included the 210 and 216, and there was also a laptop version, the SynPhonix 225, and a PCMCIA card, the P27. All are now out of production. Users still remembered the 215 decades later; in 2021 a former Smith-Kettlewell staff member listed it on a mailing list among the synthesizers he used there after the early Votrax.
Predecessors: SynPhonix 200 (Artic Technologies, 1985)
