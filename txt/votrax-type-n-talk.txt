Detailed Description:
Votrax's operator's manual describes the Type 'N Talk as a small, light case 'no larger than a portable cassette recorder', needing no more than 7.7 x 5.2 x 3.1 inches of space and weighing about 2 pounds (896 g). The Henry Ford's catalogue measures its Model 100 at 2.75 inches high, 5 inches wide and 7.5 inches long, made of metal, plastic, rubber and paper, with the label 'Model 100 - Type'N Talk' on the underside.
The front panel carries only two knobs, both on the left: VOLUME and FREQUENCY. Everything else is on the back panel. Reading from the left, the manual places the push-button ON/OFF switch at the far left, then the POWER jack, the RS-232 SERIAL PORT in the centre, the BAUD SELECT switch block and, last, the SPEAKER jack.
Power comes from a separate transformer whose cord ends in a 5-pin DIN plug; the power receptacle accepts only that connector, which keeps it from being confused with the other jacks. There is no built-in loudspeaker: the manual says to attach any 8-ohm speaker to the SPEAKER jack, and Antic's reviewer priced the unit at $375 'plus a speaker' for that reason.
Data rate is set by hand on the back panel. The manual's baud-selection table covers eight switches and says to close only one at a time. When switched on, the unit says 'SYSTEM READY'. A serial cable had to be obtained separately; Atari owners also needed the Atari 850 interface.
For a blind user the layout is simple to learn by touch, two knobs at the front, all sockets at the back, though Antic's reviewer complained that the on/off switch sat on the back and that there was no power light. Trace Center's 1987 Resource Book 3 lists it (as Type-N-Talk) among the synthesizers that educational programs of the time required.

History:
Its manual is copyrighted 1981, and The Henry Ford museum dates its unit (Model 100) to 1981. Antic magazine's review (volume 1, number 2) tested it on the Atari 400 and 800, which needed the Atari 850 interface and a cable (about $30 from the maker). It listed for $375 plus a speaker, since the unit needed an external 8-ohm speaker, from Votrax at 500 Stephenson Highway, Troy, Michigan. It spoke an unlimited vocabulary from plain English text at 300 baud and could output the phoneme codes it generated, so users could hand-tune pronunciation. The reviewer had used it for six months, but at a science fair most first-time listeners found Street Electronics' ECHO-GP ($369.95) clearly easier to understand. He also complained that the on/off switch was on the back and there was no power light.
