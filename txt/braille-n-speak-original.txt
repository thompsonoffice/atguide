Braille 'n Speak (original)
Company: Blazie Engineering
Category: Notetakers
Released: 1987 to 1990s

Overview:
A pocket notetaker with a six-key Perkins-style braille keyboard and speech output, introduced at the 1987 NFB convention for $895.

Specs:
Released: 1987 to 1990s
Processor: Not documented
Memory (RAM): 200K nonvolatile memory (1988 review)
Storage: Not documented
Operating system: Not documented
Default speech synthesizer: Votrax SSI-263 speech chip in original units (later Blazie models moved to a DoubleTalk chip)
Battery: Internal rechargeable; about 10 hours per charge (Blazie, 1988)
Display or braille cells: Not documented
Speech synthesizers available: Built-in Blazie speech only (Votrax SSI-263 chip in early units); no other synthesizers. It could also act as a speech synthesizer for a PC screen reader.

Physical Description:
A 1988 review gives about 8 x 4 x 1 inches and under 1 lb. AFB describes the device as 2 lb. Braille keys plus space bar.

Detailed Description:
The Braille 'n Speak is a pocket notetaker with a braille keyboard and speech output, and no braille display. You type in braille on six keys and a space bar, and it speaks what you typed and reads files back aloud. Deane Blazie introduced it at the July 1987 NFB convention for $895, when other blindness computers cost thousands. Its built-in Grade 2 translator turned braille into print for a printer or computer.
It is a flat, low rectangular box about the size of a videocassette. AccessWorld described it as looking like little more than a box with seven keys. Blazie's 1988 article says it fit in a coat pocket and weighed one pound; AccessWorld's 1999 interview gives 2 lb for the classic model. A 1988 review gives about 8 x 4 x 1 in. It is flat because Blazie's first wedge-shaped prototype was rejected by blind testers.
The keyboard follows a Perkins braillewriter: six dot keys in a row across the top surface, three for each hand, with a space bar nearest you; the space bar is the seventh key. Commands are chords, meaning a letter typed while holding the space bar. For example, O-chord then C opens the calculator, and E-chord executes a calculation.
The original had 200K of memory, which Blazie described as 200 to 300 braille pages. It was later described as 180K in 45 pages of 4,096 characters each. Along with notes and word processing it had a talking clock and calendar, a phone directory, a terminal mode, and a mode as a battery-powered speech synthesizer for a computer. Files stayed in memory when it was turned off, which earlier experimental notetakers could not do.
The exact port and switch layout of the 1987 classic is not documented in the sources found. Later models had a rocker power switch and earphone jack on the right side and serial ports on the left. Its color is not documented in the sources found.

Features:
Braille input, speech output
Talking calculator, clock and calendar
File storage in nonvolatile memory

History:
Deane Blazie built it after leaving Maryland Computer Services. He based it on the Kentucky PocketBraille that his friend Fred Gissoni was developing at the Kentucky Department for the Blind: Gissoni showed him the keyboard-and-processor board and said it could take notes on its own. Blazie's first prototype was wedge-shaped. Gissoni and Tim Cranmer told him to make it flat, because braille is written with flat hands. Blazie put $10,000 into building 10 units and took them to the NFB convention in July 1987, where he sold all but his prototype. Blazie Engineering was then a family business in Churchville, Maryland: his wife Marty took orders, and his sons Bryan and Chris built and assembled units. A calculator and stopwatch with countdown timer came in early 1988 as a $99 option. Updates came once or twice a year on EPROM chips; by the mid-1990s flash ROM let owners install updates from a 3.5-inch floppy. By 1999 Blazie estimated 70,000 of its notetakers were in use.
Successors: Braille 'n Speak Classic (Blazie Engineering, Name later given to the original line); Braille 'n Speak 2000 (Blazie Engineering, 1999 (APH catalog entry)); Braille Lite 18 (Blazie Engineering, Summer 1993); Braille 'n Speak 640 (Blazie Engineering, October 1991)
